1.1  Purpose & prerequisite

Track 2 turns a trustworthy, standardized regional dataset into dashboards, forecasts, and AI-assisted narration, with Responsible AI and governance woven through the track rather than taught as a single bolt-on module.   • One evolving dataset. The same regional dataset is reused module to module, so skills compound instead of resetting with each example.
  • Responsible AI woven throughout. Modules 1, 4, 6, 7, and 8 each carry an explicit data-ethics or governance callback, so the formal frameworks in Modules 9–10 land as reinforcement of habits already practiced.
  • Flexible pacing. The track compresses to a 2-week in-person intensive or extends to a 9+ week self-paced cohort with mentor office hours, without rewriting content.

Chapter 3

The regional dataset

Every earlier version of this curriculum pointed to an abstract “Track 1 capstone dataset.” This site names an actual one: a household survey from Rwanda's national statistics office, joined to administrative boundaries from the UN's own humanitarian data platform. Both are real, both are reachable without a data-sharing agreement with a specific government office, and together they carry every exercise in this track, from Module 1's first bar chart to the capstone's fairness audit.

3.1  Primary dataset: NISR microdata

The National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda (NISR) publishes household and establishment survey microdata through its Central Data Catalog, including the Integrated Household Living Conditions Survey (EICV), currently in its seventh round (EICV7, 2023–2024), the Labour Force Survey, and the Population and Housing Census. Catalog browsing is open to anyone; downloading a full microdata extract typically requires a free account and acceptance of that dataset's stated access policy, the same NADA-style pattern used by national statistics offices across the region.

For a zero-registration starting point, NISR's Data Sharing Platform and its published census indicator tables provide district- and sector-level aggregates that are enough to drive every chart, map, and dashboard exercise through Unit C without waiting on a microdata access request.

3.2  Geospatial layer: HDX administrative boundaries

The Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX), run by UN OCHA, publishes Rwanda's Common Operational Dataset for Administrative Boundaries (COD-AB): province, district, and sector polygons, each carrying a P-code. No registration is required and the license is open. This is the exact join key Track 2 Module 4 is built around, and it is the same P-code discipline Track 1 teaches, so the geospatial join a participant writes in Module 4 is not a toy exercise, it is the join a hub analyst actually runs.

3.4  Other national statistics offices across Africa

The pattern this chapter is built on, a national statistics office's microdata catalog joined to HDX administrative boundaries, is not particular to Rwanda. Most African NSOs run the same NADA-based catalog software NISR does, so a hub running this track for a different country swaps the source and keeps every notebook, join, and dashboard unchanged. Four verified examples, one per region, plus the pattern generalizes to the rest of the African Union's 55 member states.

CountryOfficeCatalogNotes

Two cross-country alternatives are worth knowing alongside the country-by-country catalogs above: the DHS Program (dhsprogram.com), Demographic and Health Survey microdata covering most African countries on a comparable indicator set, useful when a cohort wants cross-country comparison built into Unit D's forecasting exercise; and HDX's own country groups for the matching COD-AB boundary layer, keeping the P-code join pattern from Module 4 identical regardless of which country's data drives the cohort.

3.5  Population surfaces: WorldPop

WorldPop, a research group at the University of Southampton, publishes high-resolution gridded population estimates (as fine as 100m cells) for every African country, including age- and sex-structured layers. It fills a gap none of the survey-based sources above cover: a denominator. A district's raw case count from EICV or a census table means little without a population figure to turn it into a rate, and WorldPop's grids let a participant compute that rate at a finer resolution than the census's own reporting units. It is a natural population-weighting layer for Module 4's choropleths and a common data source in UN and humanitarian analysis specifically because of that resolution.

3.6  Macro indicators & cross-country microdata: World Bank

The World Bank runs two separate, complementary catalogs. World Bank Open Data serves the World Development Indicators, thousands of macro time series (GDP, poverty, health, infrastructure) for every country back decades, reachable through a documented REST API with no key required, a good fit for Module 7's forecasting exercise when a cohort wants national-level context alongside the district-level NISR series. The separate Microdata Library hosts household-survey microdata directly, including the Living Standards Measurement Study (LSMS) panel surveys run across a dozen African countries, a second microdata option alongside the national NSO catalogs in 3.4.

3.7  Regional & thematic sources

Two further sources round out what a regional hub typically reaches for. Afrobarometer is a public-attitudes survey run across more than 30 African countries, covering governance, service delivery, and trust, a useful complement when a cohort's dashboard needs a perception indicator alongside NISR's material indicators. The African Development Bank's Africa Information Highway aggregates macroeconomic and social statistics across AfDB member countries in one queryable portal, a continental-scale alternative to pulling from each country's NSO one at a time.

3.8  Web scraping for data collection

Every source above ships a bulk download or an API, which is exactly why they are listed first. Occasionally a number only exists in an HTML table on a ministry or NSO web page with no download link. A light, respectful scrape is the tool for that last-resort case, not a default way to reach for data that a catalog above already serves.

Before scraping anything

Check the site's robots.txt and terms of use. Prefer an official API or bulk CSV when one exists, every source in this chapter has one. Rate-limit requests and identify the script with a real User-Agent. Cache what is fetched locally instead of re-hitting the site on every re-run. Never scrape personal or sensitive data even where it is technically reachable: the OCHA Data Responsibility Guidelines a participant meets formally in Module 9 apply here just as much as to any other data source.

Chapter 1

Overview

1.1  Track rationale

Track 1 is the foundation the rest of the curriculum stands on. Before a regional indicator can be visualized, modeled, or narrated by an AI assistant (Track 2), it has to be pulled together from multiple source systems, harmonized against shared standards, documented so someone other than its creator can trust and reuse it, and checked for quality. This track builds that discipline, in order, because each stage depends on the one before it: data integration, interoperability standards, metadata, and data quality management.

The track draws directly on the standards the UN system already uses for this work: the Humanitarian Exchange Language (HXL) and OCHA's Common Operational Datasets (CODs) and P-codes; the International Aid Transparency Initiative (IATI) standard; the Statistical Data and Metadata Exchange (SDMX) standard; and the UN National Quality Assurance Framework (NQAF) Manual. Participants finish the track with a dataset, and the reusable pipeline that produced it, ready to feed directly into Track 2.

1.2  Design principles applied to this track

  • Python & R, side by side. Every technical module ships both languages, as in Track 2, rather than as separate tracks.
  • One evolving pipeline. A single multi-source regional dataset is reused module to module, so the Module 10 governance note and the capstone are built on the same pipeline participants have maintained since Module 1, not a fresh example.
  • Never drop, always flag. Every harmonization and validation step in this track surfaces failures for review rather than silently discarding rows, a habit that carries straight into Track 2's Responsible AI practice.
  • Flexible pacing. The 10-week structure compresses to a 2-week in-person intensive (Units A–B in week 1, C–E plus capstone in week 2) or extends for a fully self-paced cohort with mentor office hours.

Rather than one clean file, Track 1 starts from what a regional hub actually receives: three exports that describe the same ten Rwandan districts but disagree on column names and date formats. 3.2  Messy on purpose

Two rows, one in Partner A and one in Partner B, carry a deliberately misspelled district name ("Nyarugenge Dist.", a trailing space on "Rwamagana ") so Module 4's unmatched-row check has something real to catch and fix, not just describe. Every other row is generated from the exact same seeded values Track 2's dataset uses, split apart into these three shapes rather than fabricated separately, so a correctly-run pipeline reconciles them back to identical numbers.

The pattern every module's ingestion code follows: rename, deduplicate, and coerce types before anything gets merged.

3.4  The handoff, verified

Module 9's pipeline notebook does not just describe publishing the integrated dataset, it writes data/processed/track2_dataset.csv directly, then re-reads it to confirm the row count and date range. Run data/make_track1_sources.py once, work through the ten Track 1 notebooks, and Track 2's Module 1 dataset is exactly what your own pipeline produced, not a separately-provided shortcut.
